How it works

For every crag the site replays the last two weeks of weather to work out how wet the rock is now, then runs it forward through 109 different weather forecasts (from the European, German and UK weather centres). “Dry” means nearly all of those forecasts keep the rock dry for most of your session; “Maybe” means they disagree.

It knows which way each crag faces, so it can tell when the sun is actually on the rock, and how sheltered it is from wind. Rain dries off fastest in sun, wind and dry air, and slowest on dull, still, humid days. After a long wet spell the rock soaks up water and can stay damp, green or seepy even when the surface looks dry. On cold mornings warm damp air can condense on the rock (“sweating”), and when an edge is in mist or low cloud (“clag”) the droplets soak the rock even if it barely rains — the model uses forecast visibility, humidity and cloud to catch this.

Recent rain is the middle value of five weather models, which lines up well with Met Office radar. Tides and waves come from a marine model. Weather data: Open-Meteo (CC BY 4.0). Crag info: UKC, BMC, OpenStreetMap.

Limits. It can’t see individual seepage streaks, green routes or a damp crack on an otherwise dry crag. It hasn't yet been checked against real reports, and anything more than 3 days ahead is a rough guide. Always check access and bird restrictions. Source.
